My research aims to use genomics to uncover population structure and adaptive potential of organisms in 
estuarine systems. This multi-disciplinary research spans marine biology, genetics, and conversation.

## Current work

My postdoctoral work aims to design and optimize SHERLOCK assays for commercially important tuna species, 
allowing for real-time species identification. In addition to the tuna SHERLOCK testing, I optimized a SHERLOCK 
assay for Atlantic salmon that recently was used at a NOAA port for real-time species identification. 
These assays are able to use mucus swabs and lateral flow strips, enabling for in-field, real-time detection.
Utilizing the whole genome dataset that was necessary for SHERLOCK assay design, I am currently focusing on 
comparative evolutionary genomics of the tuna that were sampled. Here, I am establishing phylogenetic relationships 
and quantifying genomic heterogeneity in divergence across species. Additional research goals are to quantify standing 
genetic variation and assess each species' long-term adaptive capacity, as well as test for gene flow among species and 
determine whether it has contributed to shared adaptive variation.

## Previous work

My PhD work used genomics as a tool to study eastern oyster population structure and response to stressors, such as disease.

### Eastern oyster population genomics
Eastern oysters in Great Bay Estuary (GBE), NH showed levels of population substructure that could be generally 
classified into native oysters, cultivated oysters from the local oyster farms, and a unique collection of oysters 
at a restoration site. Additionally, when looking at clustering, subtidal and intertidal oysters separated out. 
The overall unique population stratification among native, cultivated, and restoration oyster subpopulations within 
GBE were presented to oyster conservationists, who then were able to restructure their management plan to maximize 
success at the restoration oyster site. In this case, Restoration efforts in GBE should keep in mind the importance of 
sustaining the current level of diversity to ensure best chances of survival of eastern oyster groups. 

### Eastern oyster response to disease
Oysters of both native and cultivated heritage were collected to determine differential gene expression in oysters from 
varied heritage and environmental conditions and showed molecular responses to stressors like salinity and temperature. 
Results identified candidate pathways associated with temporal tolerance and local adaptation, offering targets for 
enhanced selection when breeding. Several annotated pathways showed the underlying biological processes involved in the 
eastern oyster’s response to environmental stressors, such as ribosomal function, structural molecule activity, and 
nucleotide binding, are more conserved as common molecular mechanisms of oysters that respond to stressful scenarios.
